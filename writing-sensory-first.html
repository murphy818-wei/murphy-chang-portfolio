MURPHY LAB · STEAM IN EARLY CHILDHOOD #03

Why sensory experience comes first.

A reflection on how young children begin to understand the world.

Can young children understand the world without first experiencing it?

In my work with children aged two to four, I have come to notice something simple but important: they do not begin with concepts. They begin with their senses.

When they meet something new, they often look first, then reach out to touch it. Through seeing, touching and returning to the same experience, they begin to build their understanding of the world.

What can be missing in early learning?

Sometimes children are given an abstract idea too early, or they are told the final result before they have had time to explore. Sometimes the focus shifts towards making something that looks “right.” The finished product is visible, but the child has had little space to wonder, try or think in their own way.

What I want to protect is the process in between.

Starting with an experience

In my classroom, I try to begin with something children can meet through everyday materials. Before introducing an idea, I ask myself: How can I spark curiosity? How can I invite children into the experience?

Instead of explaining air first, we explore it through plastic bags and balloons. Instead of defining floating and sinking, children handle familiar objects, make a guess and test what happens.

When a child says, “I think this will float,” I can answer, “Let’s try.” The conversation can begin there.

If we want children to understand, we may need to slow down. We need to leave room for them to explore, experience and discover before we ask them to explain.

Children do not always understand first and then act. Often, they act, and through that process they begin to understand.
