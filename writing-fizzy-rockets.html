MURPHY LAB · STEAM IN EARLY CHILDHOOD #01

Making the invisible visible

How children began to understand air through their own questions and sensations.

Air is around us all the time. Children use it to breathe, blow and move things, but they cannot see it. I was interested in what might happen if they had a chance to build an idea about air before I gave them a scientific word for it.

So I began by asking what they thought air could do. I listened to their guesses, then asked another question: “Can we put air somewhere and keep it there? How?”

I brought out plastic bags. Some children blew into them. Others tried to scoop up air and quickly pinch the opening shut. I did not need to tell them which way was correct. Their different attempts gave us something to look at together.

After the bags were sealed, I asked the children to press on them and step on them. They felt the bags push back. That small moment mattered to me. Pressure was no longer only a word that belonged to an adult explanation. It was something they could feel in their hands and under their feet.

Before the answer, an experience

We then moved into the rocket investigation. The children tested what might change a launch, including the amount of water, the temperature and the timing of the reaction. A launch that did not work gave us a reason to stop and look again.

The children did not need to name the science immediately. They needed time to notice what the air was doing.

This is the part of STEAM teaching I keep returning to. An abstract idea can begin with a question, a material and a feeling that does not quite make sense yet.

My work was to make room for that first noticing, then listen carefully enough to decide what question could come next.
